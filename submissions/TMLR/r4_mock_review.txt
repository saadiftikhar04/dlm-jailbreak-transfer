TMLR Official Review

Submission: Cross-Paradigm Jailbreak Transfer: A Unified Evaluation of Autoregressive and Diffusion Language Models


Summary Of Contributions:

The submission studies whether jailbreak attacks developed in the autoregressive (AR) era remain effective on diffusion language models (dLLMs). It applies three existing attacks chosen to represent distinct mechanisms (PiF, token-salience flattening; ArrAttack, robust semantic rewriting; MetaCipher, cipher-based hidden-intent encoding) to three causal victims (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct; a "Llama" row that is Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct under PiF and MetaCipher and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct under ArrAttack; and the hybrid attention/Mamba-2 reasoning model Falcon-H1R-7B) and three diffusion-family victims (LLaDA-1.5-8B, Dream-v0-Instruct-7B, DiffuCoder-7B-Instruct). Prompts are pooled from HarmBench, MaliciousInstruct, JailbreakBench and StrongREJECT (913 prompts). Each original prompt contributes one final attacked prompt and one final response; PiF and MetaCipher are scored on the full pool and ArrAttack on a 165-prompt held-out stage, for 11,946 judged cases in total.

Under the attack-specific DeepSeek-based judges, MetaCipher reaches 31.9% overall ASR, ArrAttack 7.1% and PiF 6.3%. MetaCipher is highly effective on Qwen2.5, Llama and LLaDA (71.5%, 71.1%, 47.3%) and near zero on Falcon-H1R, Dream and DiffuCoder. A cell-stratified subsample of 2,982 responses is re-scored with Claude-Sonnet-4.5 under two rubrics and with GPT-5.6-sol. The authors report that the all-model ordering MetaCipher > ArrAttack > PiF is preserved under every judge, whereas the low cells are strongly judge-dependent (Dream and DiffuCoder MetaCipher rise to roughly 78-80% under Claude, which the authors trace to Claude scoring unfilled response templates as harmful). The paper argues that within-family heterogeneity (LLaDA versus Dream) dominates the causal/diffusion contrast, supports this with an 18-cell variance decomposition and a case-level mixed-effects logistic model, and concludes that a family-level effect is not identifiable with three models per family.

The paper also reports a per-victim no-attack (plaintext) control and expresses ASR as lift over that control, a benign cipher-decoding control for the diffusion victims, a final-answer-presence audit for Falcon-H1R, a matched-prompt LLaDA denoising-step bracket, a three-seed replication of selected cells, two descriptive portfolio summaries (MEE, the maximum ASR over attacks, and MDG, the max-minus-min ASR), and a four-gate conceptual account of jailbreak transfer (surface-cue perception, intent reconstruction, policy arbitration, trajectory persistence) offered as an organizing hypothesis. The contribution is empirical and methodological; no new attack or defense is proposed.


Strengths And Weaknesses:

Strengths

S1. The question is timely and relevant. dLLMs are being released at increasing scale, and whether red-teaming practice built around causal decoding carries over to them is an open and practically important question. A careful attack-by-victim matrix on public 7-8B dLLMs is a useful reference point for the community.

S2. The paper is unusually candid about the limits of automated jailbreak evaluation. It reports Wilson intervals for every cell, a no-attack control per victim, results under several judges with confusion counts and agreement statistics against a named reference judge (Table 10), and it withdraws results it cannot substantiate (the earlier human audit, the full-pool ArrAttack re-judge) instead of reporting them. This is the right instinct and is more transparency than most jailbreak papers provide.

S3. The identification of a concrete judge failure mode, namely that a StrongREJECT-style rubric run with Claude scores unfilled instruction templates ("Step 1: <main idea of the instruction> ...") as harmful compliance, is a useful and actionable observation for anyone using LLM judges on dLLM outputs, which are prone to template echoing and degenerate completions. It complements StrongREJECT, JAILJUDGE and recent judge-reliability work.

S4. Several of the controls are the right ones to run: the benign cipher-decoding control (separating decoding ability from refusal), the Falcon-H1R answer-presence audit (separating truncation from refusal), the paired McNemar analysis of the LLaDA step bracket, and the per-victim plaintext control. The fixed unit of analysis (one final response per original prompt) is sound in principle.

S5. The paper exercises appropriate restraint on the family question: it does not attach a nominal standard error to a causal-versus-diffusion effect estimated from three models per family, and it reports leave-one-model-out sensitivity of its family summaries. The LLaDA-versus-Dream contrast is a good cautionary example against family-level safety claims.

Weaknesses

W1. The victim set is not what the paper says it is. The abstract, Section 1, Section 3.1, Figure 2 ("Llama-3.1") and the Ethics section describe the causal victim as Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, but the Table 1 caption and Appendix A.5 disclose that PiF and MetaCipher were run on Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct and only ArrAttack on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. The "Llama" row therefore combines two checkpoints of different size and generation. Every quantity that compares attacks within that row (the vulnerability signature, MEE = 71.1, MDG = 62.6, the "nearly identical, MetaCipher-dominated profiles" of Qwen2.5 and Llama in Section 5.2, the model random intercept in the mixed-effects model, the variance decomposition in Table 7) compares different models, and the headline 71.1% MetaCipher number belongs to a 3B model that is not listed among the victims. It is also unclear which Llama checkpoint the 3.3% plaintext control was run on. A disclosure in a table caption is not sufficient for an error that affects one of six victims and the main-text narrative.

W2. Several of the cells that carry the paper's narrative are unstable across judges in ways the text does not acknowledge, and the paper's own appendix numbers contradict the main-text story for these cells.
- Llama MetaCipher: 71.1% under the official judge, 63.3-66.3% under Claude, 44.6% under GPT-5.6-sol, and 11.4% (seeds 9.0/12.2/13.1) in the three-seed replication under the common binary DeepSeek judge (Table 11). The same replication gives 72.0% for Qwen2.5 MetaCipher, within one point of its official 71.5%. A 60-point gap between the official result and the common-judge replication on one cell, when the neighbouring Qwen2.5 cell replicates almost exactly and the three Llama seeds span only 4.1 points, cannot be explained by seed variance. It is not discussed anywhere; Section 6.5 and D.6 report only the seed range of this cell. The claim that Qwen2.5 and Llama have "nearly identical" profiles is not supported.
- LLaDA MetaCipher: 47.3% official, but 14.5% under GPT-5.6-sol and 10.2% [4.5, 21.7] when the stored baseline responses in the matched-step bracket are re-judged with the common binary DeepSeek judge (Table 5). Under two of the four non-official measurements, LLaDA's MetaCipher ASR is in the range of Falcon-H1R's GPT-5.6-sol reading (9.0%), not Qwen2.5's (66.9%). The flagship statement that LLaDA "clusters with causal Qwen/Llama, not its diffusion family" (Figure 3, Sections 5.2, 6.2 and 7) is therefore judge-conditional in the same way the paper concedes for Dream and DiffuCoder, but it is presented without that caveat.
- ArrAttack on diffusion victims: LLaDA is 8.5% official versus 43.0% under GPT-5.6-sol, 55.2-60.6% under Claude and 38.4% in the common-DeepSeek replication; DiffuCoder is 10.3% official versus 21.2% (GPT-5.6-sol), 26.7-27.3% (Claude) and 25.6% (common DeepSeek). Table 10 shows that the official ArrAttack judge recovers only 14 of the 106 GPT-5.6-sol positives on the diffusion rows. Under GPT-5.6-sol, ArrAttack exceeds MetaCipher on LLaDA (43.0% vs 14.5%) and on DiffuCoder (21.2% vs 0.0%). The conclusion in Section 6.3 that semantic rewriting "transfers poorly" therefore rests on the official ArrAttack judge alone.
- The paper describes GPT-5.6-sol as the strict judge that sides with the official reading, but on LLaDA and DiffuCoder ArrAttack it is two to five times more permissive than the official judge, while on Llama ArrAttack it is stricter (0.0% vs 8.5%). The simple strict-versus-lenient story in Sections 1, 5.4 and 6.6 does not describe the data in Table 3.

W3. The "primary, judge-invariant" result is weaker than presented and is not the question the paper asks. The ordering MetaCipher > ArrAttack > PiF holds only for the all-model average. Under the official judge, ArrAttack (70/990, 7.1%, Wilson [5.6, 8.8]) and PiF (344/5478, 6.3%, [5.7, 7.0]) are not distinguishable even before accounting for clustering by prompt and model, so the official ordering is really MetaCipher > {ArrAttack, PiF}. At the model level the ordering is not invariant: under the official judge ArrAttack exceeds MetaCipher on Dream and DiffuCoder, and under GPT-5.6-sol it exceeds MetaCipher on LLaDA and DiffuCoder. More fundamentally, a ranking of three specific attack implementations run under unequal protocols (W4) says little about the stated research question, which is whether attack mechanisms survive the change in generation paradigm. On that question the paper's own conclusion is that the family effect is not identifiable, which is an honest answer but leaves the main finding largely negative and judge-conditional.

W4. The protocol is not as "unified" as the title suggests, and some asymmetries could favour one attack or one family.
- Final-attempt convention: Section 3.3 states that each original prompt contributes exactly one final attacked prompt and one final response, but Appendix A.3 states that "MetaCipher's causal rows concatenate up to several attempts into one stored final response". If the judge sees several attempts for causal victims but a single attempt for diffusion victims, causal MetaCipher ASR is closer to a best-of-N rate and the 31.5-point MetaCipher family gap could be inflated by construction. The number of affected rows and the effect of judging only the last attempt are not reported.
- Query budget and adaptivity: Figure 1 labels MetaCipher "cipher + RL", and A.1 describes a multi-agent attacker that plans and coordinates ciphers. It is not stated whether cipher selection uses victim feedback, how many victim queries each attack issues per prompt, or whether budgets were equal across victims. If MetaCipher is adaptive per victim while PiF and ArrAttack issue a single query, the ranking partly reflects budget, and MetaCipher is not a transfer attack in the sense used in RQ1.
- "Transfer" itself: none of the attacks is optimized on a causal victim and then transferred to a diffusion victim (PiF uses a BERT source model, ArrAttack fixed T5/MPNet/RoBERTa components, MetaCipher a DeepSeek attacker). What is measured is the application of AR-era attacks to both families, not causal-to-diffusion transfer as RQ1 states.
- Judges differ per attack (binary harmfulness for PiF and ArrAttack, a four-way categorical label requiring correct decryption for MetaCipher), so cross-attack comparisons under the official judges compare different success criteria. The appendix also gives conflicting judge settings (A.2: "default API sampling settings of the corresponding attack implementation"; A.5 and the Ethics section: temperature 0). If the official judges ran at default temperature, their own run-to-run variance is unmeasured.
- Decoding is confounded with family: causal victims are sampled once at temperature 0.8 / top-p 0.9, Dream and LLaDA are greedy, and DiffuCoder uses temperature 0.3. LLaDA's denoising budget differs fourfold between PiF and the other attacks, and the description of that budget is inconsistent (Section 6.2: 128 steps per 128-token block for PiF and 32 steps per block otherwise; A.3: "steps = gen length = block length 128 under PiF", which also implies a 128-token generation length for PiF and contradicts the stated 512-token cap for all victims). The matched-step bracket shows that the budget alone moves LLaDA MetaCipher from 10.2% to 46.8% under a common judge (18 gains, 0 losses), an effect as large as the family gap the paper discusses. No comparable sweep is reported for Dream or DiffuCoder.
- Denominators differ (913 versus 165 prompts). Since A.1 establishes that the ArrAttack pipeline is transfer-only at evaluation time, there is no methodological reason to restrict it to the held-out stage; the restriction only adds sampling noise to every cross-attack comparison. Table 11 also reports ArrAttack runs on 300 prompts per diffusion cell, which exceeds the 165-prompt held-out stage and appears to contradict the statement that the 165-prompt estimate is the only reportable ArrAttack result.

W5. The explanation of the near-zero MetaCipher cells on Dream and DiffuCoder is internally inconsistent. Section 6.4 says the cipher wrapper "actively destroys the capability to comply"; Appendix D.1 says the benign decode control shows that the hidden harmful intent "is recovered and then declined (refusal-after-decode)"; Section 6.6 and Appendix D.4 say that many of these responses are unfilled templates with no harmful content; Table 6 says the Dream failure mode is partial recovery with mask placeholders, scored as wrong decryption. These are four different mechanisms (capability loss, refusal, template echoing, decoding failure) offered for the same cells. MetaCipher's official judge is already categorical (compliance / too general / wrong decryption / refusal), so the per-victim distribution of those categories would directly adjudicate between them, but it is not reported. The benign decode control does not settle the question either: on the diffusion victims, benign requests are "complied with" more often when ciphered (Dream 71.4%, DiffuCoder 97.4%, LLaDA 94.8%) than when given in plaintext (65.8%, 49.3%, 52.1%), which suggests that the "comply" label is sensitive to producing the requested output format rather than to correctly recovering the hidden request. Decoding correctness should be measured directly before the control is used to rule out a decoding ceiling (Section 6.1).

W6. The plaintext control and lift (Delta) are computed in a way that does not support the abstract's claim that the wrapper "can suppress, not just expose, harmful compliance". The control uses 300 prompts while PiF and MetaCipher use 913 and ArrAttack 165, so Delta compares different prompt sets. The control is judged with a DeepSeek binary judge while MetaCipher cells are judged with the categorical judge, whose success definition differs and which, per the LLaDA bracket, can disagree with a binary DeepSeek judge by a factor of four; a negative Delta for MetaCipher (DiffuCoder 0.9% vs 4.3%) may therefore reflect the change of judge rather than suppression. The comparison should be paired (same prompts, same judge). The LLaDA ArrAttack control is given as 12.3% in Section 6.4 but 13.7% in Table 1. The finding that LLaDA complies more with plain requests (13.7%) than under PiF (7.0%) or ArrAttack (8.5%) is interesting, but it also means that these two attacks function as non-attacks on LLaDA in this setup, which should be stated plainly rather than folded into an ASR ranking.

W7. Most of Falcon-H1R's cells are non-measurements. With a 512-token cap, only 29.0% of PiF rows and 44.2% of ArrAttack rows contain any final answer after the reasoning trace, and only about 17% of open-ended benign prompts reach a concrete answer (D.1). Yet Section 5.2 calls Falcon-H1R "the most robust model" and asserts "this is not a refusal artifact", and Falcon's zeros are included in the causal family averages (Tables 1 and 2), the MEE/MDG figure and the mixed-effects model. A 512-token budget is not appropriate for a reasoning model. The 91.7% answer rate on XSTest-style benign prompts, which presumably call for short answers, does not address truncation on harmful prompts that call for long, multi-step answers.

W8. The statistical modelling has unresolved problems.
- Table 9 reports a diffusion-family fixed effect of -0.97 with posterior SD 0.05, while the model-within-family random intercept has SD 3.03 [1.82, 5.06]. With three models per family, the uncertainty of a family contrast should be on the order of the between-model SD (roughly 3.0 x sqrt(2/3), about 2.5 on the log-odds scale) unless strong priors are used. An SD of 0.05 suggests that the family term is not being estimated against the model-level variance (for example, the nesting is not specified as intended, or the approximate posterior is badly under-dispersed). Software, priors, inference method and convergence diagnostics are not given.
- The Table 9 caption contradicts Appendix D.3. The caption describes "standard-error-bearing family coefficients" that confirm the family effect is carried by the interactions and says that the Claude refit "preserves the qualitative sign structure", whereas D.3 says no inferential SE should be attached to the family question and reports that the MetaCipher x diffusion sign flips under both Claude judges (+1.59 and +1.63 versus -1.38).
- The random-effects structure omits attack-by-model variation, even though the attack x model interaction accounts for 27.7% of the cell-level variance in Table 7; that variance is pushed into the fixed effects.
- Section 5.7 attributes the variance shares (about a quarter each to model-within-family and to the attack x model interaction, 6% to family) to "the mixed-effects decomposition (Section 4.3)", but these numbers come from the saturated sums-of-squares partition in Table 7, which has no error term.

W9. There are many internal inconsistencies and leftovers from earlier revisions that make the paper hard to trust as a record of what was done. Examples beyond those above:
- Section 5.7 states "Exposure envelopes rank models judge-invariantly" (also the Figure 2 caption). Under Claude-A the MEE ranking is Qwen2.5 (80.1) > DiffuCoder (79.5) > Dream (78.9) > LLaDA (69.9) > Llama (63.3) > Falcon (9.6), very different from the official ranking; under GPT-5.6-sol, Dream (0.6) falls below Falcon (9.0) and LLaDA's maximum comes from ArrAttack.
- Section 5.7 states "Hidden-intent recovery is each model's largest exposure under the official judge". This is false for Dream (ArrAttack 6.1% vs MetaCipher 0.1%) and DiffuCoder (ArrAttack 10.3% and PiF 5.4% vs MetaCipher 0.9%).
- Section 5.7 states "MetaCipher exceeds PiF and ArrAttack on five of six models and is strongest under both judge sets". From Tables 1 and 3, MetaCipher is the maximum on four of six models under the official judge (counting Falcon's 0.2% vs 0.0%), four of six under GPT-5.6-sol, and six of six under Claude.
- Contribution 3 says that the low susceptibility of Dream and DiffuCoder is "confirmed under two providers", while the abstract says low cells should be read "descriptively rather than as robustness" and Section 6.4 argues that much of the "robust" diffusion cluster is attack-induced suppression rather than robust refusal.
- Section 2.5 and the Ethics section still describe a human spot-check as having been conducted (the Ethics section says its "non-blind single-annotator nature" is disclosed in Appendix B), while Section 3.4 and Appendix B withdraw it because the labels are unrecoverable.
- The introduction refers to "two DeepSeek judges" reading the Dream/DiffuCoder MetaCipher cells low, but Table 3 contains only one DeepSeek column for those cells.
- The Table 3 caption says Claude-A/B agreement is ">= 0.50" on the causal cells, but Falcon MetaCipher (a causal cell) is 0.40, and the lowest agreement is on Dream PiF (-0.02), not on the contested MetaCipher cells.
- The Table 2 footnote gives the family-gap range as "0.10 and 0.29 points"; these are proportions (10-29 percentage points).
- Undefined or stale terms: "ACTR 1.414" (Figure 3), "BehAttack-style" (A.1), "(C2) caveat" (D.1), "T2 bracket", "the previous report's 55.3%" (Table 7 caption), "the old 16% plaintext figure" (Section 6.4), "the decode arm the reviewers requested" (D.1), "the one anticipated strengthener this revision does not probe" (Section 6.1), and a double minus sign in the Table 9 caption.
The manuscript should read as a self-contained paper; revision history belongs in the response to reviewers. Combined with the unrecoverable human-audit labels and the row-key problems in the full-pool ArrAttack run (D.7), the residue of earlier versions raises a broader concern about data provenance that the authors can best address by making the row-level verdict package available to reviewers.

W10. The fidelity of the attack implementations is not established. Appendix A.3 refers to "the shared reimplementation that drives all three attacks". PiF and ArrAttack report substantially higher success in their source papers than the 6-7% observed here, and the PiF example in Figure 3(a) produces a prompt in which the harmful object is deleted and the sentence is no longer interpretable, which is hard to reconcile with PiF's similarity bound (theta = 0.85, A.1). If the reimplementation routinely destroys intent, PiF's low ASR (and its negative lift on LLaDA) reflects the implementation rather than the token-salience mechanism, and the "Gate 1" interpretation in Figure 3 does not follow. A reproduction of at least one source-paper setting per attack, together with intent-preservation statistics for the attacked prompts, is needed before low transfer is attributed to the mechanism.

W11. Smaller issues with the prompt pool and the content analysis. HarmBench's 400 textual behaviors include copyright behaviors, which HarmBench scores with a dedicated hashing-based classifier rather than a harmfulness judge, and contextual behaviors that require an accompanying context string; it is not stated how either was handled, and copyright contributes 570 cases with zero MetaCipher successes. JailbreakBench sources part of its behaviors from earlier datasets (including TDC/HarmBench and AdvBench), so the pooled set may contain near-duplicates that are counted as independent prompts. Table 4 uses MetaCipher's taxonomy for PiF and MetaCipher but ArrAttack's own labels for ArrAttack, and "Other policy" covers 390 of the 913 prompts, so the content-conditioned analysis (RQ3) is weak.

W12. A missed opportunity for a more controlled paradigm comparison. Dream-v0 is initialized from Qwen2.5-7B, and DiffuCoder-7B is adapted from Qwen2.5-Coder-7B. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct is already in the pool, and the Qwen2.5 (71.5%) versus Dream (0.1%) MetaCipher contrast is the closest thing to a same-lineage AR-versus-diffusion comparison that the study contains, but it is not analysed as such. Adding Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct would give a second pair. Post-training still differs within each pair and across the three dLLMs (for example preference optimization for LLaDA-1.5 versus instruction tuning for Dream-v0-Instruct and code-focused tuning for DiffuCoder), and the paper does not document each victim's post-training and safety tuning, which is a first-order confounder for any family-level claim.


Claims And Evidence: No

The core empirical artifact (an attack-by-victim matrix with per-cell intervals, controls and multi-judge re-scoring) is valuable, but several central claims are not supported by accurate, convincing and clear evidence in the current version. (i) One of the six victims is misidentified throughout the main text and mixes two checkpoints within its row (W1). (ii) Claims about the Llama and LLaDA MetaCipher cells and about ArrAttack's poor transfer are presented as robust findings, but the paper's own appendix shows the same cells moving by 30-60 points across judges, including between the official judge and a common DeepSeek judge (W2). (iii) Four mutually incompatible mechanisms are given for the low diffusion MetaCipher cells, while the categorical data that would discriminate between them is not reported (W5). (iv) Protocol asymmetries (multi-attempt concatenation for causal MetaCipher rows, unreported query budgets, unequal decoding budgets and stochasticity, unequal denominators) could systematically favour one attack or family (W4). (v) Several statements in Sections 5.7 and 6.4, the abstract and the Table 9 caption are contradicted by the paper's own tables (W8, W9). The authors acknowledge that absolute ASR is not identifiable without a calibrated judge; until a small human calibration is performed, claims that depend on the level of a cell (the low cluster, LLaDA's clustering with the causal models, negative lift as capability suppression) need either that calibration or substantially narrower phrasing. I believe these issues can be resolved with a focused revision (re-running or relabelling the Llama cells, a modest human audit, re-judging only final attempts, and rewriting the claims), and I would be glad to update my assessment.


Audience: Yes

Researchers working on LLM red-teaming, jailbreak evaluation methodology and dLLM safety would want to know that AR-era attacks behave very heterogeneously across public dLLMs, that within-family differences (LLaDA versus Dream) dominate any family-level contrast, and that conclusions about low-ASR cells can reverse with the choice of judge because of identifiable failure modes such as template echoing. Practitioners deploying or evaluating dLLMs would also benefit from the attack-by-victim matrix as a reference. These findings are of interest to part of TMLR's audience irrespective of novelty, provided the claims are brought into line with the evidence.


Requested Changes:

Changes critical to securing my recommendation for acceptance

1. [Critical] Fix the victim identity problem (W1). Preferably, run PiF and MetaCipher on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct over the full pool so that the row is a single model. If that is not possible, report Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct as separate victims, remove all within-row cross-attack quantities (signature, MEE, MDG, the Qwen/Llama profile comparison) for the mixed row, refit the variance decomposition and the mixed-effects model with the correct model identities, state which checkpoint the plaintext control used, and correct the abstract, Section 1, Section 3.1, Figure 2 and the Ethics section.

2. [Critical] Explain the cross-judge discrepancies on the headline cells (W2): Llama MetaCipher (71.1% official vs 11.4% common DeepSeek vs 44.6% GPT-5.6-sol, while Qwen2.5 replicates at 72.0%), LLaDA MetaCipher (47.3% official vs 10.2% when the stored responses are re-judged with the common DeepSeek judge vs 14.5% GPT-5.6-sol), and LLaDA/DiffuCoder ArrAttack (official 8.5%/10.3% vs 38-61%/21-27% under every other judge). For each, report the official outcome distribution, give the verbatim prompts of both DeepSeek judges, and show a sample of discordant rows. Then either revise the claims that rest on these cells ("nearly identical" Qwen/Llama profiles, "LLaDA clusters with causal models", "semantic rewriting transfers poorly") or show why the official reading should be preferred.

3. [Critical] Add a small human calibration. A blinded audit by at least two annotators of a few hundred rows (for example 300-500), stratified by cell and by judge-disagreement pattern, with a pre-specified rubric that separates refusal, off-target or clarification requests, unfilled templates, partial or wrong decryption, generic or too-general content, and actionable compliance, with inter-annotator agreement reported. Use it to report each judge's precision and recall against human labels and, ideally, judge-corrected ASR estimates for the low and contested cells. The paper itself states that absolute ASR is not identifiable without such a reference; the cost is small relative to the 1,412 GPU-hours already spent, and it would turn the judge comparison from a disagreement report into a calibration result.

4. [Critical] Enforce the final-attempt convention uniformly (W4). Report how many MetaCipher rows per victim concatenate several attempts, re-judge using only the final attempt (or apply the identical aggregation to all victims), and recompute Tables 1-3, the family gap and the mixed-effects model. State, per attack, the number of victim queries per prompt and whether any component (in particular MetaCipher's cipher selection) uses victim feedback; if MetaCipher is adaptive, describe it as such and do not treat it as a transfer attack. Confirm that no reported number uses best-of-N selection (as the withdrawn D.7 re-judge did).

5. [Critical] Report the per-victim distribution of MetaCipher's categorical outcomes (compliance, too general, wrong decryption, refusal), together with the fraction of responses that are unfilled templates, and use it to give a single, consistent account of the low Dream/DiffuCoder MetaCipher cells (W5). Remove or reconcile the competing explanations in Section 6.4, Section 6.6/D.4, D.1 and Table 6. For the benign decode control, measure decoding correctness directly (for example, whether the response addresses the specific hidden benign request, or whether the model can state the decoded keywords in a benign setting), and explain why ciphered benign prompts receive higher compliance than plaintext ones.

6. [Critical] Make the plaintext control paired (W6): run it on the same prompts as each attack cell (all 913 prompts, or report Delta only on the overlap), score control and attack with the same judge, and report paired tests. Until this is done, remove the claim in the abstract and Section 6.4 that the MetaCipher wrapper suppresses or "destroys" the capability to comply, and correct the 12.3% / 13.7% discrepancy for the LLaDA control.

7. [Critical] Treat Falcon-H1R appropriately (W7). Either re-run it with a generation budget adequate for a reasoning model (or with its reasoning mode disabled, if supported), or exclude it from family averages, MEE/MDG, the mixed-effects model and any robustness statement, and replace "the most robust model" in Section 5.2 with a description of the truncation regime.

8. [Critical] Correct the statements contradicted by the paper's own tables (W8, W9): the judge-invariance of the MEE ranking (Section 5.7 and the Figure 2 caption), "hidden-intent recovery is each model's largest exposure under the official judge", "five of six models", the attribution of Table 7's variance shares to the mixed-effects model, the Table 9 caption (family SE and sign preservation), Contribution 3 versus the abstract and Section 6.4, the human spot-check claims in Section 2.5 and the Ethics section, the "two DeepSeek judges" statement, the LLaDA step-budget and generation-length descriptions (Section 6.2 vs A.3), and the judge temperature (A.2 vs A.5). Remove all revision-history language, and define or remove ACTR, BehAttack, C2 and T2.

9. [Critical] Re-scope the headline claims to what survives these corrections. In particular: (a) state the attack ordering as an all-model aggregate, note that ArrAttack and PiF are not distinguishable under the official judge, and note that the ordering does not hold per model; (b) replace "transfer from causal to diffusion" with an accurate description of the design; (c) present LLaDA's resemblance to the causal models with the same judge-conditional caveat already applied to Dream and DiffuCoder. I would also suggest leading the results with the per-victim, per-judge matrix and the lift over a paired control, and moving the four-gate framework, which the paper itself labels an untested hypothesis, to a shorter discussion subsection.

10. [Critical] Given the provenance issues the paper documents (unrecoverable audit labels, inconsistent row keys in D.7), make the anonymized row-level verdict package and the analysis scripts described in the Ethics section available to reviewers during the discussion period, so that Tables 1, 3, 9, 10 and 11 can be recomputed.

Changes that would strengthen the work

11. Analyse matched-lineage pairs (W12): Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct versus Dream-v0-Instruct-7B, and add Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct as the AR counterpart of DiffuCoder-7B-Instruct. Add a table documenting each victim's base model, post-training (SFT, preference optimization, safety data), chat template and system prompt.

12. Add a decoding-order ablation within the diffusion models. LLaDA is decoded semi-autoregressively in 128-token blocks, which makes it the most AR-like of the three dLLMs at the block level, and it is also the dLLM that behaves most like the causal models under MetaCipher. Running LLaDA with a single 512-token block, varying the unmasking strategy of Dream and DiffuCoder, and extending the step-budget sweep of Table 5 to Dream and DiffuCoder would directly test the paper's motivating hypothesis about non-causal generation and would partially probe the untested Gate 4.

13. Validate the attack implementations (W10): reproduce at least one reported setting from the PiF and ArrAttack papers with the reimplementation, and report intent-preservation statistics for PiF-attacked prompts (embedding similarity to the original, and the fraction from which a reference model can recover the original request).

14. Re-run ArrAttack on the full 913-prompt pool with stable row keys and a single-attempt rule. Since the pipeline is transfer-only at evaluation time, this removes the denominator asymmetry and also resolves the status of the 300-prompt ArrAttack runs in Table 11.

15. Include at least one diffusion-native attack (for example DIJA or a parallel-decoding attack) as a positive control. This would show that the pipeline and judges register dLLM jailbreaks when they occur, and would turn the "attack mismatch, not immunity" argument of Section 6.4 from a literature citation into a result of the paper.

16. Equalize decoding stochasticity across victims (greedy decoding for all, or k samples per prompt for all with the aggregation rule stated), or report the sensitivity of causal-victim ASR to temperature.

17. For the mixed-effects analysis, report the software, priors, inference method and diagnostics, include attack-by-model random slopes, and explain the family coefficient's posterior SD (W8). Alternatively, given that the family effect is declared non-identifiable, drop the family coefficient table and keep the per-model matrix and the posterior predictive summary.

18. Improve prompt-pool hygiene (W11): report near-duplicate removal across suites, evaluate HarmBench copyright behaviors with HarmBench's own classifier or exclude them, state how contextual behaviors were presented, and use one shared content taxonomy for all attacks in Table 4.

19. Pin the judges: give exact model identifiers and access dates for the Claude (OpenRouter) and GPT-5.6-sol endpoints, include the verbatim rubric of each judge in the appendix rather than only in the reproducibility package, and, if the official judges ran at non-zero temperature, report their run-to-run agreement. Rename the "official" judges to "attack-specific (DeepSeek) judges" unless they are the judges used in the source papers.

20. Tighten the writing. The judge caveat is restated in nearly every section (1, 5.1, 5.2, 5.4, 5.7, 6.2, 6.4, 6.6, 7); stating it once and clearly, with Table 3 as the anchor, and then reporting per-judge results would make the paper considerably easier to read. MEE and MDG are the maximum and the range over three attacks; presenting them as descriptive summaries rather than as new metrics would be more accurate.


Broader Impact Concerns:

The paper includes an Ethical Considerations section that is broadly adequate for an evaluation study of public attacks, benchmarks and models: it introduces no new attack, does not release harmful completions, and sanitizes its qualitative examples. I have three smaller concerns. First, the plan to delete the full field-level responses at the end of review conflicts with verifiability, particularly since the paper already reports that an earlier human audit cannot be substantiated because its labels were lost; I would recommend retaining the raw responses under gated, logged access for qualified researchers rather than deleting them. Second, the paper should state that its use of commercial APIs to construct attacks (the DeepSeek attacker) and to judge harmful outputs (DeepSeek, Claude via OpenRouter, GPT-5.6-sol) complied with those providers' usage policies. Third, Figure 3(c) contains a stepwise outline of a time-based blind SQL injection procedure, and Appendix D.1 names other harmful outputs that were elicited; the current level of abstraction appears acceptable, but the authors should keep these examples at that level and avoid adding operational detail in revision. I do not see an ethical concern that would by itself block acceptance.

Comment: The study asks a worthwhile question and is more transparent about judge dependence than most jailbreak evaluations, and its broad message (AR-era attacks behave very heterogeneously on dLLMs, and conclusions about low cells are judge-conditional) is likely to survive revision. In its current form, however, the first TMLR criterion is not met: one victim is misidentified and mixes two checkpoints, several flagship cells move by 30-60 points across judges without discussion, the protocol contains asymmetries (multi-attempt concatenation, unreported query budgets, unequal decoding budgets) that could favour one attack or one family, and a number of main-text claims are contradicted by the paper's own tables. Resolving requested changes 1-10, in particular the Llama cells, a modest human calibration, final-attempt re-judging, the MetaCipher outcome breakdown and the rescoping of the claims, would move my recommendation to Leaning Accept or Accept.
